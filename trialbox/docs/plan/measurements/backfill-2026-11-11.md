# Back-fill memory, measured 2026-11-11 (T-305b, D-403)

What was measured: the adapter's part of a first load (read, map, pseudonymise, write the snapshot's NDJSON) as the
back-fill and as the nightly full load, on the same feed. Not measured: the OMOP load and the lake rebuild, which
still read the whole snapshot (see "What this does not show").

How: `TB_BACKFILL_SCALES=1,4,8 pytest tests/perf/test_backfill_50k.py -m perf -s`. The feed is the synthetic site A
(600 fictional patients, 2014 to 2026) stacked 1, 4 and 8 times; copy `c` has its own chart numbers and its dates 28
years earlier, so more copies means more years of history. Each load runs in its own process and reports its own peak
resident memory (`ru_maxrss`). Shared development machine (4 cores, 15 GB, other jobs running); validation off; no
lake rebuild.

| Copies | Years | Records | Back-fill peak RSS | Back-fill time | Nightly load peak RSS | Nightly load time |
| --- | --- | --- | --- | --- | --- | --- |
| 1 | 13 | 18,580 | 79 MB | 2.7 s | 139 MB | 2.5 s |
| 4 | 55 | 74,290 | 113 MB | 9.8 s | 348 MB | 9.5 s |
| 8 | 111 | 148,570 | 160 MB | 19.5 s | 630 MB | 19.4 s |

Reading it: the nightly load grows by about 3.7 KB per record (the whole night is held as resources); the back-fill
by about 0.6 KB per record. Time is the same. What still grows in the back-fill: the CSV reader reads one whole table
file before it yields its first row (the character-set detector of D-215 decodes the file at once), and the patient
list is held to apply chart-number merges. The largest table, not the years, sets that part.

What this does not show:

- the 50,000-patient, ten-year hospital of the card (TST-PERF-01). At 0.6 KB per record a 50 million record history
  would still need about 30 GB through the CSV reader; a streaming CSV reader (or the SQL source, which yields rows
  as it reads) is needed before that run, and the run itself needs a machine with the disk for it;
- the OMOP load and the lake rebuild of the published snapshot, which hold the whole snapshot in memory.
