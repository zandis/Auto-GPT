# Size test under the stub model, 2026-11-17 (card H-23, D-1137)

**Reader:** the people who build TrialBox, and the integrator deciding the machine for the capacity statement

This page is a stub-model measurement on lane H's cloud container (below), with the product code of master `57263236`
and the tool of this branch. It is not the capacity statement (T-667), and its figures are not the reference
workstation's. The ladder ran from 5,000 to 200,000 synthetic patients and from 1 to 20 studies. At each size it ran the
scale gate (ingest, lake rebuild and publish, the cascade of one study), then the orchestrator's FEAS and SCREEN jobs
study by study. Each part ran under its compose service's memory cap. The results file is `size-test-2026-11-17.json`,
next to this page.

The tool:

```
TB_ENV=DEV TB_TZ=<the site's zone> PYTHONPATH=libs:services:. python -m tools.size_ladder \
    --plan 5000:20:2 20000:20:1 50000:5:1 100000:1:1 200000:1:1 \
    --work <scratch folder> --results ladder.json --resume
```

A plan item is `patients:studies:repeats`. The data is the scale gate's synthetic hospital
(`tools/scale_gate.py`, lane B2): no real data, no network, no model. The model stand-in answers at once, so model
time is near zero here.

How to read it. The peak is the process tree's largest resident memory, in GiB (the cap in brackets after the
service). "Per unit" is milliseconds per patient (generate, ingest, rebuild, cascade) or per patient and study (the
FEAS and SCREEN marks). "FEAS x5" is the first five studies' FEAS jobs together, and so on. The SCREEN counts are of
the last study in the mark. The 20-study rungs run the eight shipped rulesets in turn (GZQO, RA-BIO, AD-MAB,
GOUT-COH, NEUR-COH, ONC-OSI, RA-COH, RA-JAK). Under the stub only GZQO and ONC-OSI send pairs to the model. The
"rebuild+publish" row is the publish's own time. The lake process's whole life is longer (the rebuild, then serving
the gate's cascade). A **FAILED** row was killed at its cap. Its seconds and peak are as far as it got.

Spread: the 5,000 rung ran twice. Each stage's two times are within 4.4 % of each other (ingest 96.4 to 100.6 s). The
larger rungs ran once, so their single times carry the same few per cent of noise at best.

## Where it first slows

By the rule of D-1137 (more than 1.5 times the lowest time per unit at a smaller size, stages of 5 s or more), the
only stage that **slows** is **FEAS**, and it first slows at **50,000 patients**: 6.25 ms per patient against 3.66 at
5,000 (1.7 times). It then keeps bending: 12.8 ms at 100,000 and 22.6 ms at 200,000. Doubling the patients made the
one-study FEAS job 4.1 times longer from 50,000 to 100,000 (312 s to 1,284 s) and 3.5 times longer from 100,000 to
200,000 (4,513 s, 75 minutes for one study). The time grows with roughly the square of the patients.

The evidence of where the time goes, measured: during the 200,000 FEAS job the lake service's process used about 4,050
CPU-seconds in its first 22 minutes (more than one core throughout) and grew to 2.8 GiB. The orchestrator's job
process used 6 CPU-seconds in all and stayed at 0.45 to 0.58 GiB. The time is in the lake's queries, not the
orchestrator.

The cause, **shown** at 20,000 patients. FEAS asks its funnel SQL through `query_by_patients`
(`services/matching/batches.py`) in D-748's batches. With the default `TB_CASCADE_S1_ROWS` of 12,000 rows and 36
month-ends, a batch is 333 patients, so the number of queries grows with the patients. Each query costs the lake time
that grows with the patients too, so the total grows with their square. At 5,000 patients (180,000 rows) D-924 makes it
one query, which fits the flat rate below 20,000. The A/B, one GZQO FEAS job three times in one process over the same
20,000-patient lake, counting the lake calls:

| `TB_CASCADE_S1_ROWS` | Seconds | Lake queries | Seconds in lake queries |
| --- | ---: | ---: | ---: |
| default (12,000) | 88.4 | 68 | 83.3 |
| 100,000,000 (one whole query) | 24.1 | 8 | 22.3 |
| default again | 84.6 | 68 | 82.6 |

One whole query is 3.6 times faster at 20,000, and the 60 extra queries hold all of the difference. The lake's memory
with one whole query was not measured here; the batches exist to bound it (D-748), so the fix is the owning lane's to
weigh.

Nothing else slows by the rule. Ingest gets cheaper per patient as it grows (19.7 ms at 5,000, 13.0 ms at 200,000).
The rebuild and publish rises slowly (5.03 ms to 7.05 ms, 1.40 times) and stays under the factor. The 5- and 20-study
marks at 5,000 and 20,000 are flat per study. A killed stage is not judged (its time is not a rate). It shows as
FAILED.

## The limits found

Each limit with its stage, size, time and memory, and the code. None is fixed here (D-1137).

1. **SCREEN is killed at the orchestrator's 4 GiB cap from 50,000 patients.** Lane **A2**. The GZQO SCREEN job (the
   first study) peaked at 4.01 to 4.03 GiB and was killed after 232 s (50,000), 299 s (100,000) and 581 s (200,000).
   The FEAS job just before it, in the same process, peaked at 0.51 to 0.58 GiB. The SCREEN job's own peak was
   0.87 GiB at 5,000 and 2.02 GiB at 20,000, about 76 KB more per patient. The studies after it at 50,000 (four of
   five) did not run.

   Where the memory is, **shown** at 20,000 patients by Python's own memory tracer (`tracemalloc`, 8 frames, the
   largest snapshot of one GZQO SCREEN job; 1.22 GiB traced at the peak). 1.0 GiB of it (5.0 million allocations) is
   pydantic models built after the cascade has ended: `_screen` in `services/orchestrator/scenarios/screen_cascade.py`
   (line 680) builds every patient's verdicts at once, `{pid: verdicts_of(...) for pid in pids}`, and `verdict_of`
   makes a `CriterionVerdict` and an `Evidence` per patient and criterion (lines 399 and 402, about 408,000 each for
   19,370 patients). The next largest are the coded values read for the evidence (`services/matching/coded_values.py`,
   line 145, 87 MiB). The cascade's S1 rows (`engine.py`) held 10 MiB at that moment. The earlier guess, that S1's rows
   held the memory, is **not** what the profile shows for SCREEN.
2. **The scale gate's own cascade is killed at the same cap from 100,000 patients.** Lane **A2** (the cascade), seen
   through lane B2's gate. It peaked at 2.76 GiB at 50,000 (ending in 418 s), then reached 4.00 GiB at 100,000
   (killed after 1,177 s) and 200,000 (killed after 1,960 s) before any model call. The gate's check
   `screen.ends_under_cap` is FAIL at both sizes.

   The cause of 2 is **still a hypothesis**: this cascade (`run_cascade`, called by the gate) builds no verdicts, and
   it was killed before any model call, so the memory is in S0 to S2. The suspect is S1
   (`services/matching/cascade/engine.py`, `_Run._s1_rows` and `_Run.s1`): it asks the lake in D-748's batches, which
   bounds the lake's memory, then turns every batch into Python dictionaries (`to_pylist()`) and keeps them all,
   indexed again by patient. The profile above was of SCREEN at 20,000, where S1 had ended before the peak; a profile
   of the gate's cascade at 100,000 was not run.
3. **FEAS time grows with about the square of the patients** (above; the cause is shown). Lane **A2** for
   `query_by_patients` and the FEAS scenario (`services/orchestrator/scenarios/feas_compute.py`, `compute`); lane
   **B2** for the lake's cost per query and its memory with larger batches. 312 s at 50,000, 1,284 s at 100,000,
   4,513 s at 200,000 for one study, with the orchestrator under 0.6 GiB and the lake under 3.4 GiB. At that
   rate 20 studies of FEAS at 200,000 is about 25 hours.
4. **The synthetic hospital generator holds the whole hospital in memory.** Lane **B2**, a test tool and not the
   product. Its peak was 4.20 GiB at 200,000 patients (about 21 KB a patient), with no cap. It is not a limit at
   200,000 on this machine.
5. **The scale gate run alone needs `TB_TZ`.** Lane **B2**, minor. `python -m tools.scale_gate` stops with "the
   site's time zone is not set" unless `TB_TZ` is set (D-315: no default site zone). Under pytest the conftest sets it.
   The tool's usage text does not say so.

Not limits on this machine: ingest peaked at 3.30 GiB of the adapter's 6 GiB at 200,000, and the lake at 3.97 GiB of
its 8 GiB (rebuild and the gate cascade's queries). The disk used per size was under 2 GB at 20,000. Lane **D**
(sizing): the orchestrator's compose default of 4 GiB is what screening reaches first. Whether the reference
workstation's install raises `TB_MEM_ORCH` is lane D's sizing question, not a finding of this test.

## What could not be reached

- **SCREEN at 50,000 patients or more, and every study after the first at those sizes.** Each run's first SCREEN job
  is killed at the orchestrator cap, and the jobs share one process, so the rungs at 50,000 (five studies planned),
  100,000 and 200,000 have one FEAS figure and no SCREEN figure. 20 studies were run at 5,000 and 20,000 only.
- **Repeats above 5,000.** The 20,000 to 200,000 rungs ran once (about 5.5 hours together here).
- **A memory profile of the gate's cascade at 100,000** (limit 2 stays a hypothesis). The SCREEN profile at 20,000
  took 22 minutes under the tracer (102 s without it, in the ladder); one at 100,000 would take hours here.
- **Why ingest differs between runs at 20,000.** The follow-ups used a scale gate run alone at 20,000 for its lake;
  its ingest took 177 s, against 328 s in the ladder's run. The reason was not looked into; the follow-ups use only
  the lake that run built.
- **A sampling profile** (no profiler is installed in this container; the CPU split above is read from
  `/proc/<pid>/stat` every 30 s).
- **The reference workstation's shape.** No figure here is a figure for 16 or more cores, 192 GB, NVMe, WSL2, or a
  served model.
